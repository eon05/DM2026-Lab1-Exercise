# Homework Report: Reddit Stock Sentiment

*I loaded the Reddit dataset with `load_dataset_tool` (`load_dataset_1`): 847 posts labeled neutral (0.0, 423), negative (-1.0, 315) and positive (1.0, 109), so positive is clearly the smallest class. Inspecting the first rows (`inspect_data_2`) showed short, slang-heavy posts with no email headers, unlike the newsgroups, and some labels look questionable: "Seeing lots of red in the ticker." is labeled neutral, although "red" usually means falling prices. There was no missing text (`check_missing_3`), but the duplicate check (`check_duplicates_4`) found 23 duplicates. The tool only reports counts, not which rows, so I checked the raw file by hand and found they were repeated bot or template posts, such as daily market links and updates. Dropping them (`check_duplicates_5`) removed 34 rows, because the tool drops every copy of a duplicated post, leaving 813; this means 11 distinct posts had been repeated. Removing even the first copy is reasonable, since these posts express no opinion. The later label counts (`binarize_19`) show that almost all of them had been labeled neutral (423 became 391), while positive went from 109 to 107 and negative stayed at 315. The length statistics (`describe_data_6`) give median lengths of 96 characters for negative, 68 for neutral and 77 for positive posts (78 overall). In the box plot, all three boxes sit low and look similar, with long outliers in every class up to 2,737 characters. Since the dataset is already small, I skipped sampling.*

![Text length by sentiment](../plots/Emily_On_homework_plot_describe_data_6.png)

*Next, I built the document-term matrix (`dtm_7`): 813 documents x 4,313 terms, 99.52% sparse. That is about 20 distinct words per post, compared with about 162 per post in the newsgroups, whose DTM had 35,788 terms for 2,257 documents. The top 20 terms by frequency (`term_freq_8`) are all stopwords ("the" 874, "to" 554, "and" 471, etc.), and none of them carry sentiment. The heatmap of the first 20 terms and documents (`dtm_heatmap_9`) has 397 of 400 cells equal to 0; as in the newsgroups (2 of 400 non-zero), the first terms alphabetically are rare number strings. For the variance filter, I first counted without removing anything. Using a threshold of 0.01 (`variance_filter_10`) would keep only 490 terms and remove 3,823 (88.6%), while 0.005 (`variance_filter_11`) would keep 792 and remove 3,521. I found 0.01 to be too aggressive for such short posts, where most words appear in only a few documents, and applied 0.005 instead (`variance_filter_12`), since it keeps enough terms to work with while still removing one-off words.*

![DTM heatmap](../plots/Emily_On_homework_plot_dtm_heatmap_9.png)

*The correlation filters for the positive class showed a much weaker signal than in the newsgroups. In `pearson_filter_13`, the highest r is only 0.156 ("nice"), compared with 0.38 for sci.med, likely because the posts are short and the sentiment labels are noisy and subjective. The most notable result is that "brazil", "atlas", "cse", "providing" and "gaining" have identical r values (0.1210 or 0.1141 in Pearson, and all 0.1276 in Spearman). That means they appear in exactly the same posts: only 2 posts, both promoting the company Atlas Lithium. So a few promotional posts were most of the list, kind of like how one poster's email signature dominated the sci.med list in the newsgroups. Genuinely positive words are present but weak ("nice", "good", "potential", "dividend"), and "green" likely means green energy here, since it ranks next to "electric" and "technology", rather than rising prices. Spearman (`spearman_filter_14`) produced an almost identical list, with "doing" and "position" replacing "real" and "spy"; both also appear in the Atlas Lithium posts. The feature correlation matrix (`feature_corr_15`) again selected only stopwords as the top-variance terms, but their correlations are weaker (strongest: "the" and "in" at 0.69, compared with 0.94 in the newsgroups). This is still a document-length effect, just weaker because the posts are short. Pattern mining on the negative class (`patterns_16`: tfidf, fpgrowth, min_sup 10 instead of 80, because short posts share fewer words) found 30 patterns, all single words. The top ones are "trump" (45), "china" (33), "economy", "tariffs" and "trade", so negative posts are tied more to a news topic (the trade war) than to negative words. "good" appears because word counts ignore negation ("not good"), and "don" and "doesn" are broken pieces of "don't" and "doesn't". Because these posts have no headers and this tool removes stopwords, the patterns are meaningful, unlike the header-dominated patterns in the newsgroups.*

*Dimension reduction did not separate the three sentiment classes. With PCA (`reduction_17`), PC1 explains 31.37% of the variance and PC2 5.83% (compared with 67% for PC1 in the newsgroups). Almost all posts sit in one tight strip near (0, 0) with all three classes mixed, while a few points stretch far along PC1. One neutral and one negative post at about 40 and one positive post at about 20. These are likely the longest posts, matching the box-plot outliers, so PC1 again mostly measures post length. t-SNE (`reduction_18`) shows one dense, round cluster in the center with all three classes completely mixed and scattered points around the edges. Sentiment is harder to separate than topics because all three classes share the same stock-market vocabulary, word counts lose negation, short posts share few words, and the labels are noisy.*

*Finally, binarizing the labels (`binarize_19`) produced an 813 x 3 one-hot table with 315, 391, 107 posts, which adds up to 813. Cosine similarity again tracked shared stopwords rather than sentiment, the same surprise as in the newsgroups. Two positive stock-hype posts (documents 8 and 9, `cosine_sim_21`) scored only 0.2004, while a negative and a positive post (documents 6 and 9, `cosine_sim_22`) scored higher, at 0.3360, because they share stopwords such as "is", "to", "the", "it" and "and". Two very short negative posts (documents 0 and 4, `cosine_sim_23`) scored exactly 0, because they share no words at all. In conclusion, some problems from the newsgroups went away: with no email headers, pattern mining found meaningful words. Others stayed: stopwords still dominate term frequency, feature correlation, PCA and cosine similarity, and a few posts still dominate the correlation filters. Sentiment also adds new problems: very short posts, lost negation, noisy labels, and a news topic (politics and trade) standing in for sentiment. Next steps would be to remove stopwords, keep negation by using bigrams (for example "not good" through `ngram_range`), or weight terms with TF-IDF.*
